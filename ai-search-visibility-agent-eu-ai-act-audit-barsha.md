## Phase 1 - System brief

**What the system does**
The AI Search Visibility Agent shows how visible a brand is online compared with its competitors. It checks two places: Google search results, and the answers an AI assistant gives to typical shopping questions (for example "best online shop for sneakers"). It then sends a written report by email. The report includes a visibility score per brand, tables, a chart, and recommended actions.

**Inputs**
A user fills in a web form (n8n form) with:
- their brand name and up to 4 competitor brand names
- a country
- up to 6 search questions
- the email address the report should be sent to

The system then collects:
- Google search results for each question (from Serper)
- AI assistant answers to the same questions (from the OpenAI API)
- recent news articles about the brands (from NewsAPI)
- passages from official SEO and AI-search guidelines, such as Google's documentation, stored in a search database (Pinecone)

**Personal data:** the main subjects are companies, not people. Some personal data is still involved: the recipient's email address, and possibly names of journalists, authors or company spokespeople that appear in news articles and search snippets.

**Outputs**
- A visibility score per brand: Google %, AI %, and overall. These are calculated in code, not by the AI.
- A written report generated by OpenAI from the collected data, including comparisons, "hidden competitors" (brands the AI mentions that the user did not list), and recommendations.
- The report as a Markdown file attached to a designed HTML email.

The system **recommends**. It does not take any action or decision on its own.

**Who is affected**
- **Directly:** the SEO/GEO managers and marketing leads who read the report and decide what to do.
- **Indirectly:** the competitor brands described in the report, and any people named in news articles.

No consumers, employees or job applicants are assessed.

**Human review**
No person checks the report before it is sent. Instead, an automated fact-checker (a LangGraph agent that also uses OpenAI) compares every claim in the text with the calculated data and fixes errors, in at most 2 rounds. The human review happens **after delivery**: the marketing lead reads the report and decides whether and how to act on it.

**Who built it**
I built it alone. It is a custom n8n workflow with a LangGraph fact-checker hosted on Render. It connects third-party services: OpenAI (language model), Serper, NewsAPI, Pinecone, Gmail (SMTP) and QuickChart.

**Who would use it in production**
SEO/GEO managers and marketing leads at online brands, or an SEO agency running it for its clients. They would use the reports to plan content and SEO work.

## Phase 2 - Risk tier classification

| Question | Your answer |
|---|---|
| Does this system fall under any prohibited category (Article 5)? | **No.** It does not manipulate people or exploit vulnerabilities, score people's social behaviour, use biometrics, recognise emotions or predict crime. It scores **brands' search visibility**, not natural persons. |
| Does this system operate in any of the eight Annex III areas? | **No.** It is not used in biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, essential services or credit, law enforcement, migration, or justice and democratic processes. It is also not a safety component of a regulated product (Annex I). Its area is marketing analytics. |
| If Annex III: does it "significantly influence" decisions in that area, or is it narrow/preparatory? | **Not applicable**, because no Annex III area is involved. |
| Does this system interact with end users or generate content requiring disclosure (Article 50)? | **Partly.** It is not a chatbot: users fill in a form and receive a report, so Article 50(1) (telling people they are chatting with an AI) does not clearly apply. However, the report text is **generated by an AI**, so Article 50(2) may apply. Under that article, providers of AI systems that generate synthetic text must mark the output as AI-generated in a machine-readable way. An exception covers AI that only assists standard editing, but my report is mostly AI-written, so the exception is unlikely to apply. Article 50(4) (disclosing AI text published to inform the public on matters of public interest) does not apply, because the report is an internal business document. |
| First-pass risk tier | **Minimal risk**, with **one possible transparency duty** (marking AI-generated text under Article 50(2)). |
| One-sentence justification citing the specific article or Annex entry | The system analyses brands for marketing purposes, so it matches no Article 5 practice and no Annex III area. Its only AI Act touchpoint is that its report text is AI-generated, which may require machine-readable marking under Article 50(2). |

**Why "minimal" is backed by evidence, not a default:** I checked each prohibited practice in Article 5 and each of the eight Annex III areas against what the system actually does (see the table above). The system's output is about companies, it makes no decision about any person, and a human decides what to do with the recommendations.

**Ambiguity to flag for legal review:** whether Article 50(2) applies to an internal business report built on a third-party model (OpenAI), and what marking is technically required. The European Commission is preparing guidance and a code of practice on marking AI-generated content. The exact application dates and any transition rules should also be checked.

**Cross-check:** run the system through the European Commission's [EU AI Act Compliance Checker](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/eu-ai-act-compliance-checker) and note the result here.

**Checker result:** *"Your AI system is likely not covered by the AI Act as it does not fall within the scope of Article 3(1)."* In other words, the checker suggested it is not an "AI system" at all.

**My view - I disagree with the checker and keep my classification.** Article 3(1) defines an AI system as one that operates with some autonomy and **infers** from its input how to generate outputs such as content, predictions or recommendations. Parts of my agent are rule-based code: the scores and comparisons are calculated in code. That may be why my answers led the checker to "not an AI system". But three parts clearly infer outputs using a large language model (OpenAI): the AI answer check, the report writing with recommendations, and the LangGraph fact-checker. The workflow also runs autonomously from one form submission to the delivered email. So the system as a whole is an AI system under Article 3(1).

This is a useful lesson. The checker's result depends on how each question is answered, and a system that "only writes text" is easy to under-classify. The difference does not change the practical outcome much: whichever answer is right, there are no high-risk duties, and the GDPR and accuracy gaps still apply. If the checker is right, though, Gap 1 (Article 50) would fall away. **This is a point for legal review.**

## Phase 5 - Gap analysis and remediation plan

**Gap 1 - AI-generated report text is not marked as AI-generated**
- **Obligation:** Article 50(2), transparency for AI systems that generate synthetic text (if it applies; see Phase 2).
- **Current state:** OpenAI writes the report, which is sent as a Markdown file and an HTML email. I checked the sample report (`reports/zalando_report_2026-11-12.md`). The email and file have **no visible statement and no machine-readable tag** saying the text was AI-generated.
- **Required state:** the output should be marked in a machine-readable format and be detectable as AI-generated. It is also good practice to tell the reader clearly.
- **Remediation:**
  1. Add a visible line at the top of the email and the report: *"This report was written by an AI system (OpenAI) from measured search and AI-answer data, and checked by an automated fact-checker. Scores are calculated in code."*
  2. Add machine-readable metadata: a `<meta name="ai-generated" content="true">` tag and a generator note in the HTML email, and front matter in the Markdown file (`ai_generated: true`, `model: <model name>`, `generated_on: <date>`).
- **Escalation needed?** **Yes, to a lawyer.** They should confirm whether Article 50(2) applies to an internal business report built on a third-party model, which marking standard to follow, and the application dates.

**Gap 2 - Limitations are documented for developers, not for report readers**
- **Obligation:** Article 4 (AI literacy). The deployer's staff need to understand what the AI output can and cannot tell them.
- **Current state:** the important limitations are listed only in the GitHub README. The report reader does not see them. For example: the AI visibility check uses the OpenAI API, not the ChatGPT app; ranks only compare tracked brands; brand matching is name-based; each report is a one-day snapshot.
- **Required state:** people who act on AI output should get enough information to interpret it correctly and not over-trust it.
- **Remediation:** add a short "How to read this report" box to every report, with 4–5 plain-language limitations and the date of the snapshot. Add a one-page user guide for clients.
- **Escalation needed?** **No.**

**Gap 3 - Comparative claims about competitors are sent without human review**
- **Obligation:** parallel law, not the AI Act: consumer protection and unfair-competition rules on misleading comparative claims (in Germany, the UWG).
- **Current state:** the report makes AI-written statements about named competitor brands, such as "ASOS is invisible on Google" or "hidden competitor OTTO". It is emailed automatically. The only check is an automated LLM fact-checker, and the hidden-competitor detection is known to miss brands. If a client copies these findings into public marketing (for example "We're #1 in AI answers"), the claim may be misleading.
- **Required state:** comparative claims used publicly must be accurate, verifiable and not misleading.
- **Remediation:**
  1. Label every report "Internal analysis - snapshot of [date], not for public claims".
  2. Add an optional **"review before send"** mode. The report goes to an analyst first, who approves it in n8n with a Wait / Approve step before it reaches the client.
  3. Keep the raw data with each report, so every claim can be traced back to it.
- **Escalation needed?** **Yes, to a lawyer**, if clients plan to use report results in advertising.

**Gap 4 - Personal data and international transfers are not covered**
- **Obligation:** parallel law, not the AI Act: GDPR. This covers transparency (Art. 13), data minimisation (Art. 5), processor agreements (Art. 28) and transfers outside the EU (Chapter V).
- **Current state:** the form collects an email address with **no privacy notice**. News and search snippets can contain people's names. The data is sent to US-based services (OpenAI, Serper, NewsAPI, Pinecone, Render, QuickChart). Reports are sent from a personal Gmail account. There is no defined retention period for n8n execution logs or reports.
- **Required state:** a privacy notice at the point of collection, only the data that is needed, data processing agreements with every processor, a valid legal basis for transfers (e.g. EU-US Data Privacy Framework or Standard Contractual Clauses), and a retention period.
- **Remediation:**
  1. Add a short privacy notice to the n8n form.
  2. Strip author names from news data before sending it to OpenAI.
  3. Sign or accept data processing agreements with each vendor and record the transfer basis.
  4. Set n8n execution log retention (e.g. 30 days).
  5. Use a business email domain for sending reports.
- **Escalation needed?** **Yes, to a data protection officer or privacy lawyer.**

## Phase 6 - Compliance memo

**To:** Head of Product, BrightCart GmbH (fictional client, online fashion retailer)
**From:** Barsha Mishra, AI Consultant
**Date:** 16 November 2026
**Subject:** EU AI Act check - AI Search Visibility Agent

**1. Classification**
The AI Search Visibility Agent is **minimal risk** under the EU AI Act. It analyses brands for marketing, makes no decisions about people, and does not fall into any banned use (Article 5) or high-risk area (Annex III). The one open question is a **transparency duty**: because the report text is written by AI, it may need to be marked as AI-generated (Article 50(2)).

**2. Who is responsible**
- **Provider:** I am the provider, because I built the system.
- **Deployer:** your marketing team uses the reports, so BrightCart is the deployer.
- **OpenAI** supplies the AI model and has its own obligations as a model provider.
- **Serper, NewsAPI, Pinecone, Render** and the email services are data and infrastructure suppliers. They matter for data protection, not for the AI Act.

**3. Key findings**
1. **AI label missing.** Reports do not say they were written by AI, either visibly or in the file's data. This is easy to fix.
2. **Data protection.** The tool collects an email address without a privacy notice, and sends data to US-based services without documented agreements.
3. **Risk of misleading claims.** Reports compare you with named competitors and are sent automatically. If these findings are used in public marketing, they could count as misleading comparisons.

**4. Recommended next steps (in order)**
1. **This week:** add a visible AI-generated notice and machine-readable tags to every report. Add a "How to read this report" box and an "internal use only" label.
2. **Before rollout:** add a privacy notice to the form, put data processing agreements in place with all vendors, and set log retention. **Review this with your data protection officer.**
3. **If reports will feed public claims:** switch on "review before send", and have a **lawyer** check any advertising claims.
4. **Ongoing:** give the team a 30-minute briefing on the tool's limitations (AI literacy). Recheck the classification if the tool's purpose changes, for example if it starts scoring people instead of brands.

**5. Caveats**
This memo is a first-pass consulting assessment based on the system's documentation. It is **not a legal opinion, not a conformity assessment and not a certification**. Whether Article 50(2) applies, and from which date, should be confirmed by a lawyer. GDPR questions should be confirmed by your data protection officer.

## Reinforce

**Components I glossed over at first**
- The **"AI answer check"** is itself an AI inference. I treat OpenAI's API answer as a stand-in for what "AI assistants" say, but it is not the ChatGPT app, and the answers vary from run to run. This does not change the risk tier. It does make Gap 2 (explaining limitations) more important.
- The **fact-checker is also an LLM**, so the "fact-checked" label could make readers over-trust the report. That supports adding a human review option (Gap 3).
- **News articles** bring in personal data (names) that I had not considered part of the system (Gap 4).

The overall picture stays **minimal risk**, but the parallel GDPR and accuracy issues are bigger than I first thought.

**A design decision I would change**
Sending the report **automatically by email with no human approval step** felt efficient for a demo. In a client setting it creates the misleading-claims risk in Gap 3. I would build an approve-before-send step from the start, and use a business sender address instead of personal Gmail.
